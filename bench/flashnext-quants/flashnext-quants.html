Flash-Next Quant Map

A 125B-total / 6B-active model on one 128 GB box. Which checkpoint you pick decides both whether it fits and how fast it decodes — and those two are set by different parts of the file.

“Ours” on this page means three distinct things. Everything below was produced on one DGX Spark — GB10, sm_121, 128 GB unified memory — running vLLM 0.1.dev20073+g8e685d198 on bare metal, no container.

ours · checkpoint
A public checkpoint whose safetensors shards we rewrote on this box. Nothing is retrained and nothing is recalibrated — weights are requantized in place. fp8head is lovedheart’s NVFP4-FP8 with lm_head quantized to FP8 here.
measured
We ran it: our box, our harness, both arms of any comparison on the same day.
external
Someone else’s published number, kept in its own row because engine, PLE handling, speculation and clock policy differ simultaneously — not comparable to a measured row.

Individual figures still depend on prompt length, concurrency and whether speculation was on; those conditions are stated with each table rather than assumed.

The counter-intuitive part

At single stream the experts are 96% of the parameters and 20% of the per-token bytes — properly NVFP4, and only 10 of 512 are read. The dense path is the small half of the model and dominates the clock. This reverses under load — see below.

Sharper still: GDN linear attention costs 1.95 GiB per token — more than all ten active experts together (1.24). The largest single item in this model is not the MoE.

Why the hyper-connections resist

The model hardcodes quant_config=None on all three projections — patched, and now upstream as a commit. The 128-divisibility is a constant in vLLM's code, but it is a real limit for the fast CUTLASS blockwise kernel: every hyper-connection shape fails it (out%128 = 4 or 64, in%128 = 64), so blockwise FP8 falls to a slow Triton path. Per-channel FP8 has no block constraint and slices cleanly across the fused [320, 4, 12] = 336 layer. We built it. It changed nothing — see below.

Position decides composition

lm_head is evaluated once per draft token, so quantizing it compounds with speculation. Everything else is read once per step, so it competes.

Whether the experts matter depends entirely on concurrency

The dense scaffolding is read once per step and shared by every sequence in the batch. The experts are not: each additional sequence routes to its own ten of 512, so the expert term grows with concurrency while the dense term is amortized away. The answer to “do the experts dominate?” flips at c = 4.

Two consequences we had not been reasoning about correctly. First, our headline number has always been c = 1, which is precisely the regime where the experts matter least — every dense lever we pulled was aimed at the 80% that only exists at single stream. Second, it explains the aggregate scaling: throughput rises just 4.6× across 16× the concurrency, because bytes per token fall only 4.0× as the growing expert term eats the amortization.

input-len 4000, output-len 512, --max-num-seqs 16, temp 0.6. 6 requests at c=1, 32 at c=16. Measured noise floor: six identical k=2 runs gave 34.7–37.1 tok/s — a 6.9% spread, so a single run carries about ±4.6% at 95%. Differences below roughly 10% are not callable from single runs, here or anywhere else on this page.

Speculation does not hurt at saturation — it stops paying. +35% at single stream; at c=16 the difference is inside the noise floor and so is not measurable at all. It still cuts TTFT by 30% there, which is the reason to keep it on for agent work regardless of what it does for throughput.

k=2 and k=3 cannot be told apart on this box. A single k=3 run gives 36.8, inside the 34.7–37.1 range six identical k=2 runs produced, and above their 35.7 mean. An earlier version of this page called k=2 the optimum by comparing k=3 against 38.0 — the top of k=2's own spread. That was an endpoint-versus-spread error and the claim is withdrawn. What is solid is counter-based rather than timed: k=3 accepts 2.471 tokens per iteration against k=2's 2.133, so the third draft step buys 15.8% more tokens and returns no measurable throughput. The mechanism is named in the log: Fused multi-step draft decode is not supported by attention backend QWEN38_FLASH_NEXT_EXP_QSA_STATE; falling back to rebuilding attention metadata between draft steps. That rebuild is charged per draft step, so each extra speculative token costs more than the last — an inverted U with a low peak. QSA carries a stateful decode backend and the fused path has no implementation for it; this is a gap in the model family's support, not a misconfiguration.

Aggregate throughput is mostly a statement about prompt length

Prefill is charged to the wall clock that aggregate t/s divides by, so the same server on the same checkpoint reports wildly different “throughput” depending on the prompt:

Neither row had recorded its input length. An aggregate t/s figure without a stated prompt length carries almost no information.

Why: an “A6B” model was moving 11 GB per token

The experts are quantized properly and cost almost nothing. The dense parameters — attention, GDN projections, lm_head, shared expert — are read in full on every token, and most checkpoints leave them in BF16. They are 79% of the bytes and 67% of the parameters.

RadixArk (dense BF16) 10.98 GB
lovedheart (attn+GDN FP8) 7.25 GB
+ lm_head FP8 (ours) 6.61 GB

At GB10’s 273 GB/s these set a floor of 40.2, 26.6 and 24.2 ms per token respectively. Three groups are not per-token reads and must be excluded from this arithmetic: the vision tower, embed_tokens, and the MTP drafter. We over-counted our own roofline by including them.

Why nobody had quantized lm_head

It is BF16 in every published GPU checkpoint. Not because it is unsafe — we measured +11% at no quality cost — but because three independent pieces of vLLM plumbing block it, and any one alone is sufficient.

1 The model never passes quant_config to ParallelLMHead So UnquantizedEmbeddingMethod is installed unconditionally and the checkpoint’s declaration is never consulted.
2 config.json is authoritative, and its ignore list contains lm_head Editing hf_quant_config.json — the obvious file — changes nothing. The field you read for placement is the field you must write.
3 The vocab loader asserts every tensor is vocab-shaped assert loaded_weight.shape[output_dim] == self.org_vocab_size is true of the weight and false of its [1940, 20] block-scale companion.

Does a quantization lever compose with speculation?

It depends on where the layer sits relative to the speculative loop — and we got this wrong at first, publishing a flat “they are substitutes”.

LeverMTP gain at c=1MTP gain at c=16Why
dense projections → FP8+67% → +23% +2% → −6% read once per step — both amortize the same read, so they compete
lm_head → FP8+23% → +40% −10% → +8% evaluated once per draft token plus verify — ~3× per step at k=2, so they compound

Acceptance is unchanged across both (mean accepted length 2.15 → 2.21), so this is pure speed rather than better drafting. Quantizing the head flipped speculation from a net loss at high concurrency to a net gain.

A constant that blocks a layer class on every GPU

ModelOptFp8PbWoLinearMethod refuses any layer whose dimensions are not both divisible by 128. We assumed that reflected the kernel. It does not — TritonFp8BlockScaledMMKernel declares no shape constraint at all and passes the block size through as a runtime list; the Triton kernel takes group_n/group_k as runtime arguments. Cutlass validates only the activation group shape.

1 The 128 lives in one class constant _WEIGHT_BLOCK_SIZE = (128, 128) — one line makes a layer class unquantizable everywhere, not just on sm_121.
2 Blocks need not be square (320, 10240) wants (64, 128); (10240, 320) wants (128, 64). Assuming square silently handles half the tensors.
3 But fusion is a real blocker The down-projection is a MergedColumnParallelLinear of [320, 4, 12] = 336. A blockwise scale cannot span a fusion boundary when one shard is 4 wide — no block size divides 4, 320 and 12 together.
Checkpoint shapes are not runtime shapes

We reasoned about (320, 10240) read from the checkpoint headers for a day. The layer vLLM actually builds is 336 wide, and that number appears nowhere until the error message. Same class of mistake as reading hf_quant_config.json when vLLM reads config.json: the artefact you inspect is not the artefact the code consumes.

A lever we measured and rejected

shared_expert is eligible for the same proven scheme, worth 0.47 GB/token, and quantized by four other projects — none of which published what it costs. We measured it: +1.9% at c=1, −2.8% at c=8, for +2.06% NLL. Unlike the dense projections (−1.8%) and lm_head (−0.6%), both noise with mixed signs, that regression is directional. We are not shipping it.

And the roofline over-predicted, which matters for every projection here

0.47 GB of a 5.61 GB budget is 8.4% of the bytes and should have bought ~8%. It bought 1.9%. The roofline assumes every byte removed is a byte the GPU was waiting on — true for the dense projections at (10240, 2560), false for a (640, 2560) small matmul that is latency-bound. The roofline is an upper bound whose tightness depends on matrix size; we had been reading it as a predictor.

Three attempts on the hyper-connections, three nulls, and the error underneath

A profile put 25% of GPU kernel time in cutlass_80_wmma_tensorop_bf16 — Ampere-generation kernels on a Blackwell part — and identified them as the hyper-connections. That looked like the largest remaining lever on the box. Everything aimed at it missed:

All arms n=6 from empty compile caches, against a measured noise floor of 6.9%. Kernel dispatch was verified at the call site, not assumed — an earlier round of this measurement was invalid because a stale compiled graph replayed pre-patch code.

An earlier version of this page blamed a prefill-contaminated profile. That was wrong, and testing it is what settled the matter. The first trace was taken over --input-len 4000, i.e. 16,000 prefill tokens against 2,048 decode tokens, so the suspicion was reasonable. A decode-only re-profile — 12-token prompt, 600 generated tokens, warmed first — produces essentially the same ranking:

Decode-only trace: 9.65 M events, 14.7 s span, GPU busy 89.8%, 643 k kernel launches.

So the hyper-connections really are about a quarter of decode GPU time, the GPU really is busy, and three interventions on them still changed nothing measurable. Those three facts are all solid and they do not yet reconcile. What we can rule out: the layers are not mis-ranked, the GPU is not idle, and the kernels provably dispatched.

The live hypothesis is narrower — that FP8 does not actually speed up these shapes. The per-channel path routes (10240, 320) at M ≤ 8 through scaled_mm, and a K of 320 is small enough that halving the bytes may buy nothing against a warp-level BF16 WMMA kernel that is already latency-bound rather than bandwidth-bound. That is measurable directly, per shape, without touching the server — and it is the next thing to measure rather than assert.

Profile the regime you intend to measure. A decode-truthful profile needs a short prompt and a long generation, warmed first so JIT and cudagraph capture stay out of the trace. A profile over a realistic serving mix answers where does this workload spend time — a different question from what raises decode tok/s.

Do not use the published metrics files to compare checkpoints

gsm8k_metrics.json and aime26_metrics.json are byte-identical (sha256 88766f7e…, same latency_seconds to ten decimals) across a plain NVFP4 build, an FP8 dense-quantized fork of it, and a 512→448 expert-pruned variant. The model field inside names the original build. Hash them before letting them inform a decision — we repeated those figures ourselves before checking.

Two environment variables, both mandatory, neither documented

A VLLM_USE_DEEP_GEMM=0 DeepGEMM’s gate accepts the whole 120 capability family, which sm_121 satisfies — so vLLM routes blockwise FP8 to a kernel that then faults with CUDA error: unspecified launch failure during the startup profile run. Falls back to Cutlass and works. vllm#54125
B VLLM_GDN_DECODE_KERNEL=triton With FP8 GDN projections the default CUDA kernel hangs the engine at concurrency ≈32 — no error, requests simply stall.

Both are reachable only through blockwise-FP8 weights, so they appear exactly when you adopt the checkpoint class that helps most.

Two defects that decide whether the model is usable, and no speed test can see

Found after a full day of throughput work, on a server that had been benchmarking cleanly throughout.

Tool calling was rejected outright. The launcher set --reasoning-parser qwen3 and nothing else, so every request carrying a tools field returned HTTP 400. Adding --enable-auto-tool-choice --tool-call-parser qwen3_xml fixes it: 32/32 across temperatures 0.2 / 0.6 / 1.0 and default, correct function name every time. --tool-call-parser qwen3 is not valid — the reasoning parser is qwen3, but the tool side registers only as qwen3_coder and qwen3_xml, two names for the same class.

--max-model-len 8192 could not hold the model's own reasoning. One code task emitted 31,115 characters of thinking before 12,931 of content. 8192 was chosen for benchmarking and was never going to serve real work.

Why neither is visible to any measurement on this page

Throughput, acceptance, NLL, divergence and coherence tests never send a tools field and never run a generation long enough to overflow the window. A serving config has capabilities, not just speed — probe both before benchmarking a new recipe.

The concurrency ceiling is real, and we nearly blamed our own cap

Every concurrency number here was taken at c=16 against --max-num-seqs 16 — the cap bound exactly where we measured, which makes a flat aggregate indistinguishable from a queue. Raising the cap to 64, changing nothing else:

Both moves sit inside the 6.9% noise floor. ~100 tok/s at c=16 is a bandwidth ceiling, not a cap artifact, and c=32 buys +10% aggregate for 4× the TTFT — the wrong trade for agent work, which we rank by TTFT.

A separate recipe reports 96–109 tok/s aggregate at SEQS=16 on the same hardware with a different checkpoint, speculation setting and prompt length. Two independent configurations landing on the same number is much better evidence for a ceiling than either alone. Their published 1.2–2.7× SEQS win came from raising a baseline of 2 slots, not 16 — the same curve, below its knee.

The 266.8 tok/s figure is a different configuration

An earlier uncapped sweep reached 266.8 tok/s at c=48. That was the baseline checkpoint — its c=1 row is 17.1 — with speculation off and short prompts. It is not comparable to the 110 above, which is the shipped config at 4000-token inputs, and reading the two side by side manufactures a regression that does not exist.

The MoE backend axis, and a lever that was never untried

SM120 and SM121 have 99 KiB (101,376 B) of shared memory per block, not the 228 KiB "Blackwell" figure — that is SM100/B200 only. CUTLASS's StageCountAutoCarveout assumes the larger budget, picks ~6 stages, and overflows on the Pingpong schedule. It surfaces as a device-side assert at nvfp4_blockwise_moe.cuh:78, which is the next cudaMallocAsync sync point and not the faulting line.

A startup deadlock we were winning by luck

Four independent reporters — GB10, GX10, and two discrete-VRAM Blackwell cards — hang at warmup_kernels with PLE CPU offload enabled, the offload thread parked in _copy_cuda_inputs and the worker idle because the request is never sent. We have never reproduced it, on this exact code, across many restarts, with and without speculation.

The cause is visible in the shipped tree without any hardware. PleOffloadConnector allocates one _input_ready_event for the whole connector, on a stated assumption that only one request is ever outstanding. Under async scheduling — on by default; "mtp" sits inside EagleModelTypes so speculation does not disable it — two batches are in flight, and _launch(#2) re-records that same event behind forward #1's PLE semaphore wait. The offload thread then waits on a record the model stream cannot reach until it sends request #1, while the main thread's device-wide synchronize() waits on the D2H stream.

That explains what a PCIe-latency account cannot: unified memory reproduces it (the copies are cheap; the wait hangs), eager only narrows the window, and an air-gapped box serves for two days then hangs on the third identical boot. Upstream replaced the shared events with a per-request pool. Backported here: serves in 680 s, c=1 decode 38.1 / 36.5 / 34.8 against a 36.45 ± 1.04 reference — no cost.

Prefix caching does nothing below 1600 tokens

This model forces the attention block size up to match its Mamba state page: "Setting attention block size to 1600 tokens to ensure that attention page size is >= mamba page size", then pads the mamba page by 0.88% so the two are exactly equal. A prompt shorter than one block therefore has no full block to cache.

The counters say so plainly: 0 hits against 77,254 queries across every benchmark on this page. Repeating an identical ~1400-token prompt four times produces zero hits; a ~5,700-token prompt hits from the third request onward. The delay to the third is a separate, documented quirk of this box — the first repetition never hits — which is why any check of this needs at least three requests, not two.

What this means for the numbers here, and what is still open

Nothing on this page benefited from prefix caching, because benchmark prompts are unique per request. Agent loops reuse prefixes and would — above 1600 tokens. Whether a cache hit changes the answer on this architecture is a live question, raised by a report of an all-zero restored Mamba state on a build that conflates the 16-token attention block with the 1600-token state block. Ours equalizes them instead, so we are probably not exposed; a controlled on/off determinism test is running and this section will be updated with its result rather than its expectation.

What the field settled, and what it did not

  • SGLang cannot serve this model on GB10. Its QSA decode resolver gates on is_sm100_supported(); classic FA2 is absent and has no Blackwell kernels, so it falls through to a flash-attn-4 CuTe path that will not compile there. The reporter verified the flashinfer kernel itself runs correctly on SM121 at 4.70e-04 max abs diff — a gate, not a kernel, exactly like the four layers of "installed but not running" documented above. Base support and the resolver fix are both unmerged; the fix does cover GB10, since the gate it adds tests major 12.
  • NVFP4 KV is closed, on a third independent measurement: 44.0 tok/s against 56.8–58.6 for fp8_e4m3 on SM121. Note what that says about the alternative — fp8 and bf16 are roughly speed-neutral, so FP8 KV buys pool size, not throughput.
  • FP8 KV has a known collision with QSA: the FA4 decode call receives BF16 queries with FP8 K/V and asserts that all three dtypes match. That is precisely the plumbing an FP8-QSA-KV build needs, failing in another stack — worth a source read before a build.
  • llama.cpp's own support merged on 2026-08-27, subsuming two community patches. It is a separate implementation; the vLLM one still exists only on its PR branch.

A claim of ours that this checking withdrew

Hours earlier we wrote that a llama.cpp recipe's 415 tok/s prefill at 32k beat "our ~320 tok/s". That 320 was not ours. It came from dividing a ~100 s at 32k figure belonging to the Qwen3.8-27B work and applied to this model. We have never measured Flash-Next prefill at 32k. What we have is 4,000-token inputs at TTFT 1.69–1.94 s. The conclusion that we were behind on prefill was manufactured out of a borrowed number, and is withdrawn — as is the ranking in the other direction, since the depths still differ.

Three things worth taking from it regardless:

  • An NVFP4 PLE table exists, ships and loads, through a vLLM plugin and a public container digest. At ~26.8 GiB against the ~47.7 we run at FP8, that is roughly 21 GiB of unified memory back. The size was already on our map; a working checkpoint was not.
  • Their prefill is flat with depth — ctx_pp stays between 1,231 and 1,600 tok/s from 4k to 100k, while decode decays hard (16.2 → 1.5 at c=10, d100k). Flat prefill is a target shape for a depth curve we have never run.
  • They run PLE offload without --distributed-executor-backend mp, using a 900 s ready timeout instead. Either their build carries the uniproc fix or the timeout hides the startup race — worth knowing before anyone calls mp mandatory.

Kernel work elsewhere: QSA on llama.cpp

A separate single-Spark repo publishes a QSA CUDA patch for llama.cpp — fused ggml_get_rows_mean with RMS weighting, __ldg half2/float4 loads on the lightning WMMA path, a compact FA gather at topk=2048, and indexer Q padded 4→8 so lightning reaches WMMA. It roughly doubles long-context decode: 11.35 → 18.73 tok/s at 64k and 5.60 → 11.55 at 229k, with TTFT essentially unchanged. They also publish what they reverted, which is the half most repos omit.

Two of their results bear on ours directly. Acceptance is batch-geometry dependent: same binary, draft and prompt, -b 512 -ub 128 gives 33.3% acceptance while -b 2048 -ub 512 collapses to 3.7% and the target output diverges. Our own open acceptance gap — 56.6% here against 73.7% and 75.6% reported elsewhere — may be a comparison of numbers that were never measured under the same geometry. And speculation loses at long context on their fast kernels: MTP at 229k ran slower than the same kernels plain autoregressive, and cost 8.4% and 6.7% of prefill at 16k and 32k.

A method we are adopting from them

They lock output hashes before making any speed claim and label every row measured under a different protocol as not comparable. We verify checksums on weights; they verify hashes on outputs. Theirs is the guard that would have caught our corrupt-shard episode, where size-correct byte-corrupt shards produced fluent garbage that was invariant to every configuration change.

Measured on one NVIDIA GB10 (DGX Spark, sm_121, 128 GB unified), vLLM 0.1.dev20073+g8e685d198, PLE CPU offload, 8192 ctx, no speculation unless stated. Decode is clock-sensitive on this hardware — GB10 parks bandwidth-bound decode at ~82% of max SM clock (2411–2522 against 3003 MHz) and locking does not move it, so absolute figures are not comparable across projects.
