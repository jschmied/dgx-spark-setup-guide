NVFP4 Periodic Table

Nine public quantizations, some 1,900 possible recipes, four axes that measurably move anything — and one finding that reorders the rest: which NVFP4 you pick costs almost three times as much fidelity as the decision to use NVFP4 at all. Nine cells are now measured; all three built to test the model's extrapolation broke it, and the fourth axis collapsed to noise the moment it was measured directly.

The map

The kernel is a property of the column, not of a cell: W4A4 forces CUTLASS, W4A16 forces Marlin. It is deliberately not repeated in every cell — at equal head size both deliver the same decode rate, and labelling all sixteen would imply a per-cell effect the measurements rule out. Where the kernel does show is prefill: 6.4 s against 8.4 s TTFT.

Columns are the element, rows are its isotopes. A column fixes the body — the same weights, the same kernel, the same chemistry. Moving down a column changes only the lm_head: 2.54 GB, then 1.27, then 0.64, and finally 0.64 again with its activations in 4-bit too. Same element, heavier or lighter nucleus. Most of the isotopes here do not occur in the wild — they were synthesized by swapping a head onto a published body, which is also why three cells are marked transuranium: nobody publishes them, they only exist because we made them.

Rows are the lm_head, ordered by its distance from BF16. It holds 4.6 % of the parameters yet sets the decode rate alone, because under speculative decoding it runs once per draft position while the body runs once per step. Columns are the body.

Two things the axes do not show. The A8/A16 in a row name is the head's activation precision, not the body's — and the column's A4/A16 describes only the MLP. Attention tells a third story that neither axis carries: in every checkpoint of the grid — published and synthesized alike — the attention and linear-attention projections are W8A8, FP8 weights with dynamic FP8 activations. Read from each config_groups. It never varies there, so it cannot be an axis; but it means no grid cell is a 4-bit model in the sense the column headers suggest.

The strip period below is where that stops holding: those checkpoints target every Linear and so quantize attention to 4-bit as well. That is the only structural difference between them and the grid, and it is why their quality cannot be derived from the grid's axes.

Why there are two 2.54 GB rows. Storage width and head precision are separate axes, and exactly one checkpoint separates them: ours-w4a16 carries an FP8-precision head at full BF16 width — 2.253 % relative L1 from the pristine head, against 2.248 % for a simulated per-output-channel FP8 e4m3 round-trip (0.438 % from that simulation itself). Reading the two wide rows against each other gives the axis-saturation result directly: the pristine head measures −1.27, the FP8 one −1.11 — a perfect head is worse. Reading down the FP8 column gives the storage cost: −1.11 at 22.2 tok/s wide, −1.29 at 27.0 tok/s narrow — 0.18 pp for 4.8 tok/s.

Both 2.54 GB rows were long thought to be the only grid rows DFlash2 could draft for, because stock vLLM refuses a quantized target head and every narrower row quantizes it. That restriction is gone. Deleting the eight-line guard makes every row here draftable, and the six cells measured on 19 Aug prove it: quantized heads draft at 38.6 to 41.2 tok/s, faster than any unquantized one. The guard never described the method — see the box below. On stock vLLM the ordering still held, and there the first-party Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B-FP8 release runs it at 28.8 tok/s — 60 % over its own 18.0 with MTP — because the legacy fp8 path leaves ParallelLMHead alone (vllm#40999 asks for opting into quantizing it). Lifting the restriction for compressed-tensors heads is open upstream (sgl#35462, sgl#35496).
And the requirement is an implementation artifact, not a property of the method. The candidate selector needs logits to rank, and it reached for the head's raw weights — which a quantized head does not expose. A public recipe routes it through the generic interface instead, calling quant_method.apply(lm_head, hidden, None) — the quantization scheme's own GEMM, which every scheme implements. Nothing is dequantized, so the head stays at 0.64 GB; hidden states are chunked at 256 tokens, logits truncated to org_vocab_size, and tp_size = 1 is required. It serves RadixArk/…-NVFP4, whose head is NVFP4 — the exact case both engines refuse unpatched. Reproduced here on vLLM on 19 Aug: the same eight lines deleted, and NVFP4 heads draft at 38.6–40.6 tok/s.

That is the same class of defect as the vLLM one above: code reaching past the interface at a concrete layer type. It also named a lever this map never touched — ranking the top-k in FP32 rather than in the head's own precision, credited there with lifting accept length from 3.01 to 3.24, about 8 %. It does not reproduce here. Tested both ways on the same NVFP4 head, same 20 contexts, 19 Aug: 4.26 either way — 38.6 tok/s with and without, and on the FP8 production head +0.1 %. Whatever that 8 % came from, it was not the ranking precision on this stack. The patch is left in because it costs nothing, not because it earns anything. Their caveat matches our own measurement: "its output hash does not match speculation-off byte for byte." So the eleven cells marked as refused are refused by today's code, not by the method.
The derived cells failed every honest test, and the failure is locatable. Three cells were built to check whether the axes keep adding up away from the points that define them: unsloth-nvfp4head (said −1.67, measured −2.06), ours + NVFP4 head (−1.47 → −1.81) the W8A16 head (−1.11 → −1.29) and the wide FP8 head on a W4A4 body (−1.31 → −1.51). All four optimistic, by 0.18 to 0.39 pp — as much as the largest single axis. The first two both involve the NVFP4 head: starting from r0b0tlab (−1.69, static) and applying the measured "static → dynamic = +0.22 pp" predicts −1.47, but the measurement is −1.81 — −0.12 instead of +0.22. With a coarse head, finer activation scaling stops helping. The axes are not orthogonal there, which is why the last cell in that row is anchored on its measured neighbour instead: −2.06 plus this row's own dynamic→static delta (+0.12) gives −1.94, not the model's −1.89. Where a row has measurements, derive from them.
The once-blocked W8A16 cell is measured, and it is not the optimum. Reaching it meant working around three defects — two in vLLM (#48856, still open) and one in humming-kernels 0.1.10, which cannot compute GPU bandwidth on GB10 at all and was fixed by the vendor in 0.1.12. The result: −1.29 pp at 27.0 tok/s, against the plain FP8 head's −1.33 at 26.4. 0.04 pp and 0.6 tok/s — noise. The head's activation precision is not where the loss lives; its weights are, and W8A8 needs no patched vLLM.
Speculation is not orthogonal to this map — it inverts it. Every speed figure in the grid is vLLM with MTP n=3, where head bytes set the decode rate and the wide BF16 head is the slow row. Swap the drafter and that ordering breaks: DFlash2 reaches 36.5 tok/s, faster than any cell here. Stock vLLM refuses a quantized lm_head, which appeared to restrict it to the top row; removing that guard opens every row, and the quantized heads then decode faster than the wide one — up to 41.2 tok/s. Measured on vllm#52816 + #52883, same target, same 20 contexts: MTP n=3 20.8 → DFlash2 n=7 36.5 (+75 %); the pristine-head row measures 37.5, and the first-party FP8 release 28.8. Those all sit on a W4A16 body and pay ~9.6 s of prefill for it. Putting the same wide head on a W4A4 body gives 35.2 tok/s at 5.6 s — 1.3 tok/s slower to decode, 4 s faster to first token, and the only point measured here that is fast at both ends. Against the production checkpoint under MTP that is +32 % decode and −12 % TTFT at the same fidelity (−1.51 vs −1.53). n=7 is not the optimum — that reading compared unpaired medians on paired data (36.5 against 36.4). Paired, n=11 wins 11 of 20 contexts, and total tokens over total decode time puts it 1.7 tok/s ahead, finishing the corpus in 91 s against 103. Every DFlash2 figure on this page is measured at n=7, so the column is a floor — about 7 % low if the sweep transfers. For reference the previous best was SGLang + DSpark at 30.1 tok/s; SGLang cannot run DFlash2 yet (two merges short).

And it is the same checkpoint that holds the best fidelity in this field. At −1.11 pp and 36.5 tok/s it dominates every other cell on both axes at once — better than Unsloth's −1.53 and faster than its 26.6. The whole premise that fidelity must be bought with throughput holds only as long as the drafter is fixed. One caveat keeps this out of production: DFlash2 departs from unspeculated output measurably further than MTP does at equal draft depth (0.469 vs 0.722 similarity), and it runs on an unmerged PR.

Refit on all ten measured cells, two of the four axes collapse. The head axis is flat everywhere except NVFP4: BF16, FP8 stored wide, FP8 stored narrow and FP8 with quantized activations all sit inside 0.08 pp of each other, while NVFP4 drops 0.5 pp below them. The strongest form of that: a head that was never quantized at all is not better. On the W4A16 body it measures −1.27 against −1.11 for an FP8 round-trip; on the W4A4 body −1.56 against −1.51 and −1.53. Two independent bodies, and the pristine head loses both times — and under DFlash2 as well, 34.7 tok/s against 35.2 at identical TTFT. The drafter, like the decoder, responds to the head's size and not to what is in it: two heads of the same width land within 0.5 tok/s whether or not they ever passed through a quantizer. And static → dynamic activations, published above as +0.22 pp from a single pair, is +0.03 pp across ten — noise. What is left is one real quality axis (do not put the head in NVFP4) and one real speed axis (the body's kernel sets prefill, the head's bytes set decode).

One column now tests this end to end. All five heads were measured on a single body (unsloth's W4A4): pristine BF16 −1.56, FP8 stored wide −1.51, FP8 narrow W8A16 −1.60, FP8 narrow W8A8 −1.53, NVFP4 −2.06. The four non-NVFP4 heads span 0.09 pp against the predicted ≤ 0.08, and NVFP4 sits 0.50 pp below them against the predicted +0.50. Three of those five are builds that exist nowhere else.

Leave-one-out over the ten cells puts this fit at ±0.13 pp mean error, 0.27 worst case — against the 0.18 to 0.39 pp misses recorded above, which were derived when only four to six cells were measured. More measurements did make the derivation better. They also removed its basis in one place: the W4A4 · static column has no measurement at all, so its four cells now carry a question mark instead of a number.

The activation axis went the same way. A8 → A16 was derived at +0.22 pp and was the reason the W8A16 cell looked worth chasing. Measured head-to-head on the same body it is +0.04 pp — noise. Two of the four axes therefore saturate at FP8: neither wider weights nor wider activations buy anything back. What the head loses, it loses in its weights, and only NVFP4 storage loses enough to matter.

The two metrics disagree at this scale. On NLL the unquantized head is closer to BF16, as it must be; on top-1 it is behind. Top-1 counts argmax flips and gets loud wherever the first two candidates nearly tie. Below about 0.2 pp, trust NLL.

The two extra periods

Four checkpoints quantize deeper than the MLP, and they used to sit in a footnote because they broke the grid's two axes. They now sit below the main block, the way the lanthanides do — their own periods, only the occupied positions drawn. Reading the strip is reading a third axis: how far past the MLP the quantization reaches. With 48 of 64 layers being gated DeltaNet, that axis runs straight through the recurrent state path.

One cell is not one thing

The grid names a row by the head's precision. That is enough for FP8 and not enough for NVFP4, and the difference is measurable without serving anything: dequantize each head back to BF16 and compare it against the untouched original. Pure tensor arithmetic, seconds, no GPU.

Three independently produced FP8 heads land on the same number to three decimals. That is not luck: E4M3 carries three mantissa bits and takes one scale per output channel from the absolute maximum, so the quantization is essentially forced — there is a right answer and everyone finds it. Craft has nowhere to enter.

NVFP4 leaves room. One mantissa bit and two levels of scaling — an FP8 scale per group of 16, plus a global FP32 — means real choices: how the group scale is set, whether values are merely rounded or fitted, how outliers are handled. r0b0tlab's head reconstructs about 3 % more accurately than ours. So the NVFP4 row is under-specified: behind one cell sit at least two builds of different quality, and the better one is not ours. The FP8 rows can stay one cell per combination; that row cannot.

This also cleans up a comparison made carelessly earlier in this document: r0b0tlab's published checkpoint (−1.69) against our NVFP4-head build (−1.81) differs in head and body at once and says nothing about either alone. The weight-space figures above isolate the head; the clean body comparison is our own head on two bodies, −1.81 against −2.06. Ranking other published NVFP4 heads this way costs about 1.3 GB each — the header trick used for placement is not enough, the tensors themselves are needed.

Head bytes do not have a single effect — and this is where the map stops generalizing. Under speculation the decode rate factors into two terms: verification steps/s times accept length. Hold the activation path fixed at A16 and halve the head, 1.27 GB to 0.64: on Unsloth's body that buys +0.78 steps/s, exactly as fewer weight bytes should. On ours it costs −0.23. Hold the size fixed instead and move A8 → A16: +0.78 steps/s on our body, −0.05 on Unsloth's. Neither factor has a body-independent sign.

So "head bytes set the decode rate" is not a law here, and neither is its repair. An earlier reading of four of these cells had bytes buying steps and precision buying length, each cleanly; the two W8A16 cells break both halves. The likely reason is that A8 and A16 are not the same arithmetic at two resolutions but different kernels — W8A8 against Humming's W8A16 — whose relative cost depends on what else is competing for bandwidth. That makes head precision not an axis at all, but a kernel choice wearing an axis's label.

One thing does survive on every pairing: accept length is not a property of the head. The same 0.64 GB NVFP4 head accepts 4.26 on one body and 4.06 on the other; the same FP8 A16 head, 4.46 and 4.23. The drafter's hit rate depends on the target's weights — which the two-axis reading did not anticipate, since the body was supposed to own prefill and nothing else.

Which cell is fastest for agent work

Every speed figure on this page is a decode rate, and for agent work that is the wrong number to rank by. An agent turn is a long prompt and a short completion: across the 20 real agent contexts replayed here the median completion is 130 tokens (range 60–256) against a median context of 9,138 (range 2,008–31,228). At ~40 tok/s those 130 tokens take about 3 s, while the prefill takes 5.6 to 9.6. What you wait for is the prompt, not the answer.

The tok/s ranking is not merely imprecise here — it is inverted. The fastest cell by decode rate (41.7) is fifth of seven per turn. The slowest row of all, RadixArk at 30.1 tok/s, takes first place — it decodes 28 % slower than the leader and still finishes the turn first, on a 5-second prefill advantage. TTFT is 52–75 % of the turn, and no plausible decode advantage outruns that.

RadixArk wins on both terms at once, which nothing else here manages: the fastest prefill (4.5 s) and the second-fastest decode (40.3). It is also the least faithful cell on the page at −2.19 pp — the same property seen from the other side, since its head quantizes weights and activations to 4-bit. It moves the fewest bytes and keeps the least of BF16.

Why the figure is normalized rather than measured. Wall time as it actually occurred is not comparable between runs — and not because one drafter talks more. Paired over the same 20 prompts the median difference in completion length is +1 token, and six of the twenty hit the 256-token cap in each run alike. What moves is the median itself: bootstrapped at n = 20 these completions give a 95 % range of 104–252 tokens, so two runs of the same model can differ by 35 tokens in median output for no reason at all. That lands straight in a measured turn time — in one cell it turned a real 10 % gain into an apparent 3 %. Holding the completion at 130 removes it. The result is an index for ranking, not a duration to quote: real turns run 60 to 256 tokens, and a measured turn averages about 13 % longer than the index because long contexts carry both a high TTFT and a long completion.

One comparison the cells do not support. Within a cell the MTP and DFlash2 lines come from different engine builds: MTP from vLLM 0.27.1, DFlash2 from the #52816 branch at 0.26.1rc1, the only build that runs it. That branch's own MTP is slower — 20.8 against 22.2 tok/s on the same target — so reading the two lines against each other understates DFlash2. Where both were measured on the branch the saving is 19.5 %, against 17.5 % for the mixed pair. Compare cells along a row or column, where the engine is constant; treat the within-cell gap as a floor.

Unifying the two engines was tried, and did not work. 0.27.1 already ships DFlash; adding DFlash2 took two modules and four hooks, and it runs — same engine banner, sane output. But it decodes 5.7 % slower with 0.24 less accept length than the branch, identically on both bodies, and repeat runs reproduce to ±0.01 so that is real. Three of the four missing pieces were found only by diffing qwen3_dflash.py in full: none of them contains the string "dflash2", because they are changes to DFlash1 that DFlash2 depends on. One is a defect in 0.27.1 in its own right — the drafter is never told the target's RoPE layout, which the branch calls out as silent: acceptance falls, nothing errors, output stays correct. Restoring it recovered 0.08 of the 0.24. The rest is a context-parallel-entangled kernel guard that cannot be lifted cleanly, so the port was abandoned and the caveat above stands.

Where the crossover sits. A faster body wins only once the completion is long enough to pay back its prefill:
N > ΔTTFT / (1/rslow − 1/rfast)
For the two bodies measured here that is (9.6 − 5.6) / (1/38.6 − 1/41.7) = 2,077 output tokens. Real agent turns emit 130 — a factor of sixteen short. At 1,000 tokens the W4A4 body is still ahead (31.5 s against 33.6); the two only draw level at 2,000.

And the gap widens as a session runs. TTFT grows with context, and not at the same rate on both bodies: below 8k context it is 2.0 s against 4.1 s, at 15k and above 15.3 s against 23.8 s. The penalty grows from 2.1 s to 8.5 s per turn. Since an agent session accumulates context monotonically, the slow-prefill body loses ground with every turn it takes.

Stock vLLM and SGLang both refuse a quantized lm_head, so that looked like the one requirement deciding eligibility. It is not a property of the method — the guard is removable in eight lines and every quantized head measured here drafts fine. What the map does not say

Fidelity is not capability. The reference continuation was generated by BF16, so a model that mimics BF16 wins by construction — including where BF16 is wrong. A task benchmark could separate the two, but a paired McNemar test over 100 SWE-bench instances resolves roughly 12 percentage points, while 0.2 to 0.4 is what is at stake here.

Unsloth's calibrated KV scales are outside the model. It is the only checkpoint shipping 32 k_scale/v_scale; every other one silently falls back to a scaling factor of 1.0 under --kv-cache-dtype fp8. That advantage is baked into its measured −1.53 and missing from every derived cell.

How this was measured

Placing a checkpoint costs about 200 KB, not 25 GB. The column comes from config.json — but read every config_groups entry, not the first: in mixed-precision releases the first group is the FP8 attention group and the NVFP4 body is the second, which will put the checkpoint in the wrong column if you stop early. The row comes from the head tensor's dtype, and that can be read remotely: fetch model.safetensors.index.json, find the shard holding lm_head.weight, then two HTTP Range requests — eight bytes for the header length, then the header itself. BF16 means an untouched 2.54 GB head, F8_E4M3 means FP8, U8 with a weight_scale_2 means NVFP4. Validated against a checkpoint we hold locally: the remote read returns the same dtypes as the file on disk.

Depth was read from each config.json, counting exceptions rather than looking for them — the distinction matters. sakamakismile targets every Linear, and its six spared attention entries all sit in mtp.layers.0 — the MTP head, not the model — so the main attention stack is fully 4-bit. Its lm_head survives in BF16 only because it is named once in the ignore list; that single line is what makes a checkpoint DFlash2-eligible without a patched engine, and it is worth checking before anything else. Inferact is built the same way, and additionally spares the whole GDN — 240 entries against sakamakismile's 96 — which is the difference between the two strip rows. huginnfork targets every Linear too but spares 68 attention and 336 GDN entries — everything — which makes it an ordinary MLP-only checkpoint, and it has moved up into the main block as the published occupant of a previously derived W4A16 cell. A first pass here checked only whether exceptions existed and put both in the wrong place. The last period holds the integer quantizations — AWQ, GPTQ, INT8. They serve on this stack and all four leave lm_head free, so they draft with DFlash2 even unpatched; but their weights are integers, not NVFP4, so the column headers above them mean nothing and their cells are placed by download rank, not by scheme. They are here because leaving them out would suggest this stack cannot run them. gittensor/…-RTX5090 was gated when this page was first written and has since been opened. Reading its tensors moved it: its lm_head carries weight_scale_2 and input_scale, so the head is NVFP4 with 4-bit activations — not BF16 as the README implied — and it now sits beside RadixArk in NVFP4 · A4. It still differs from RadixArk in one way the grid cannot show: it targets every Linear, so its attention is 4-bit too, and its activations are static where Unsloth's are dynamic. None of the strip is measured: quality there is unknown, not derived — the axes that produce the grid's derived numbers do not extend to attention or GDN.
